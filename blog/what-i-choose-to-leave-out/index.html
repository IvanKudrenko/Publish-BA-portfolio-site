What I Choose to Leave Out

October 11, 2026 · 3 min read

I like simple things with a clear purpose. A notebook and a pen are a good example. I pick them up when I want to write, and I can start writing. I do not need to open an app, choose a new note, or check which tool is selected.

There is also something I like about the physical experience. I feel the pen moving across the page. The notebook does not need to imitate writing on paper. It already is paper, and I know what to do with it.

I love using my tablet, too. I write math and literature notes on it at school, and I use Notability. Changing colors, highlighting, and recording audio are useful features. At one point, I thought a tablet could completely replace paper. I do not quite think that anymore.

Sometimes I want all those possibilities. Sometimes I just want to catch a thought before it disappears.

On a tablet, the path to writing can involve a few small decisions. I need to open the right app, find the right place, and choose the right tool. Sometimes I accidentally switch to another function. Sometimes an interface changes, and I have to work out where something has moved. None of these things has to be a big problem on its own. But while I am doing them, I can lose the thought I wanted to write down.

For me, the notebook leaves less friction between a thought and its record. That is part of why I enjoy it. It has a clear job, and it does that job without having to make room for many unrelated things.

This makes me think about the features I would rather leave out of a product.

An extra feature can make sense. An eraser on a pencil is probably my favorite example. When I write with a pencil, I may want to erase something. Usually, that would mean picking up a separate eraser. Having one on the pencil supports what I am already doing. A pen that lets me change colors can be useful for the same reason.

These things have more than one function, but the functions belong together.

Now imagine an eraser with a speaker. It could play music, but why would I need music to come from the thing I use to erase? Or imagine a flashlight on headphones, or a pen that also works as scissors. There might be a particular situation where one of these combinations is useful. But adding a possibility does not explain why it belongs there.

Combining functions can also mean making compromises. I want to understand what the main purpose loses when something else is added. Does the object become harder to use? Do I have to learn more before I can do something simple? Does a new feature pull me toward a different activity?

The same questions apply to software. A feature may be useful, but its controls and settings can still make the main action harder to reach. I appreciate extra tools when they follow the same path as what I am already doing, remain optional, and do not make the basic action more complicated.

I keep returning to this while building BlinkClip, my app for batch video tasks. I am still deciding how universal it should become. Adding a finished intro to a video has a clear purpose. But should the app also include a tool for creating that intro? That could be useful, but it would bring another set of tools, settings, and decisions into the app. I have not settled that question yet.

For BA, this is becoming part of how I want to approach design. I try to start with a problem and then find a feature that addresses it. If I start with a feature, I can always imagine some way to use it. That alone does not tell me whether it should be part of the product.

I want each addition to have a clear reason to be there. I also want to notice when keeping something out allows the main purpose to work better.

That is what I like about the notebook and pen. They are ready for the thing I picked them up to do. When I consider a new feature, I want to ask: does this help someone continue what they came here to do, and what will they have to give up for it?
